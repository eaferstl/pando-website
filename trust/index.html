Trust Center

Last updated November 1, 2026

PandoCore runs on your Kubernetes nodes, so you should know exactly what it does with your data, what privileges it holds, how it behaves when things break, and where we stand on formal compliance. Here is the honest picture, including the parts that are not flattering.

What we collect, and what leaves your cluster

The node sensor reads kernel-exposed counters about the processes of the workloads on its node (memory, process, and network statistics from the host /proc filesystem, mounted read-only), then turns them into normalized drift signals. It does not read your application's data, memory contents, network payloads, environment variables, or secrets.

Stays in your cluster. A detection produces a structured evidence record written to the sensor's stdout, visible in your own pod logs, and emitted as a Kubernetes event on the workload it concerns. The optional network audit that records remote IP addresses is off by default.

Goes to PandoCore (portal.pandocore.io). Two things reach us on a default install:

  • A licensing and billing heartbeat, roughly weekly, plus a one-time cluster registration. These carry your license key, generated node and cluster identifiers, and the sensor version, but no detection data.
  • Detection evidence. By default the sensor forwards evidence records to the portal, through the cluster controller. You can turn this off completely with a single Helm value, or leave forwarding on but tell the portal not to store it. Either way, concrete identifiers inside a record (file paths, remote IPs, image digests) can be redacted before the record leaves the node.

Goes only where you point it. Beyond the portal, evidence is delivered to the destinations you configure: your SIEM (Splunk, Elastic, Datadog, syslog), a Slack channel, or your own webhook. Nothing is sent to a third party you did not set up.

What is watched, and what is not

Selection is opt-out, which is the reverse of the retired sidecar. Pods on a node are watched by default, and the exact label pandocore.io/protect: "false" excludes one. The match is case-sensitive and literal, so "False" and "0" do not exclude anything. If you are carrying the old opt-in label from the sidecar era, note that it no longer selects anything: protect: "true" is a no-op on the node sensor, and removing it does not stop a pod being watched.

Four exclusions are not policy and an operator cannot opt a namespace back into them: PandoCore's own pod, pods sharing the host PID namespace, pods sharing the host network namespace, and a pod carrying its own protect: "false". Your cluster's managed system namespaces are excluded too.

A coverage limit worth knowing before you hit it. Each sensor runs a bounded number of per-workload engines, 70 by default. On a node packed beyond that, the pods past the limit get no engine and are genuinely unmonitored rather than partially monitored. The limit is raisable to 500, and at a typical 15 to 20 pods per node it does not bind, but it is a hard ceiling rather than a soft one. The sensor publishes the count at pando_node_sensor_pods_unmonitored{reason="cap"}, and in that metric family cap is the one value that means something is wrong rather than something was deliberately excluded. If you run dense nodes, that is the number to alert on.

Failure modes

Out of the box the sensor runs in monitor mode: it observes and records, and does not block, isolate, or delete anything. Containment is something you opt into, and on the free tier it is not available at all.

It is designed to fail open. If the portal is unreachable, if its Kubernetes permissions are missing, or if the sensor itself crashes, your workloads keep running. The sensor observes from its own pod rather than from inside yours, and it is not in the path of your traffic, so it cannot take your application down with it.

The strongest response available is isolating a pod with a NetworkPolicy that denies egress except DNS, and it acts out of band through the Kubernetes API. Nothing intercepts your syscalls or network traffic inline.

A repeated attack against the same pod can resolve to an alert rather than an isolation. The first attack's own samples are folded into the baseline that judges the next one, so a second identical attack is scored against a baseline that has partly learned it. We measured this: the same injection against the same pod isolated in 69 seconds on one day and only alerted on the next, and the second attack was no less intense than the first. What moves is the response, not the detection: in the soak runs, each attack was detected and produced a signed evidence record, and alerting is the floor the sensor never drops below. A fix for this is in test and is not in this release.

It cannot delete a pod, and that is enforced rather than promised. The sensor's ClusterRole does not grant pods: delete, so termination is unreachable whatever a license says, and the code caps its own response ceiling at isolation to match. Pod termination existed only in the retired sidecar deployment mode. If you want the stronger guarantee, you can read the ClusterRole in the chart rather than take our word for it.

The sensor requests 250m CPU and 256Mi of memory per node, and is limited to 2 cores and 1Gi. Those are per node, for the whole node, not per workload. Size your instance types with the request in mind: the chart deliberately refuses to install rather than leave a node silently unmonitored, so on a node that cannot allocate the request the Helm install stops and names the node instead of reporting success.

Evidence integrity

Each evidence record carries a SHA-256 digest over its canonical contents, along with the previous record's hash and a sequence number. Together these form a hash chain: altering or reordering a record changes its digest and breaks the link, so tampering is detectable and the chain is independently verifiable. Signing makes tampering visible; it does not prevent it.

Every record is also signed with an Ed25519 key that the sensor generates in its own process. The private half of that key never leaves that process. It is never written to disk or logged, and nothing is provisioned from outside, so there is no signing key for us to distribute or leak.

The blast radius changed when the sensor moved to the node, and it changed for the worse. Under the retired sidecar each pod signed with its own key, so a compromised pod exposed one key covering one workload. One sensor per node means one signing key per node, covering the records of every workload that sensor watched. That is a real reduction in isolation, and it is the main thing we gave up in exchange for the smaller resource reservation, the removal of the admission webhook from pod creation, and the coverage a per-pod detector could not reach.

To let anyone check those signatures, the portal attests each sensor's public key against your license, using the same key that verifies PandoCore licenses. A third party can then verify a record with a key they already trust. Verification is anchored to the moment each record was written, so archived evidence stays verifiable long after the short-lived attestation that covered it has rotated.

Signing happens immediately and unconditionally; the portal attestation attaches once the sensor can reach the portal, and this too is fail-open: during an outage, evidence keeps flowing, signed but not yet attested. Because attestation is independent of evidence forwarding, you can stop sending us evidence entirely and still get the full cryptographic guarantee.

Elsewhere, the sensor uses SHA-256 to baseline process integrity, and verifies license, provenance, and SIEM-configuration artifacts against Ed25519 signatures.

What retention does and does not mean. Your plan states a retention window, 7 days on Free, 90 days on Growth and 13 months on Enterprise. That is a window records are kept for, and it is a floor rather than a deletion schedule. It is not a query interface: the portal shows recent events, and a long-range evidence query path is not built yet. If you need to hold evidence for an auditor over a year, keep forwarding it to your own SIEM as well.

Supply chain

Every production image is signed, and you can check it yourself. The release pipeline signs all five images it publishes with cosign, by digest rather than by tag, so a signature always covers the exact image you run and cannot be moved onto a different one by retagging. Signing uses a Cloud KMS asymmetric key, protection level HSM, algorithm EC_SIGN_P256_SHA256. The first signed production release is v2.0.0, published on 2026-11-06. Each signature names the release it came from, so a verified image ties back to a tagged build rather than to our word for it.

Each published image carries two software bill of materials attestations, one SPDX and one CycloneDX, generated with syft. All five images are signed and attested.

The verification key is published here rather than handed to you out of band. The public half of the signing key is committed at deploy/keys/cosign-public-prod.pem, and it is reproduced below so you can diff the two against each other. We sign with a key and do not publish to a transparency log. That is a deliberate choice, and it changes the command you need. Signatures are never uploaded to Rekor, so a bare cosign verify goes looking for a log entry, finds none, and fails. That failure is the expected result and not a broken signature. Skip the log check explicitly:

cosign verify \
  --key cosign-public-prod.pem \
  --insecure-ignore-tlog=true \
  us-central1-docker.pkg.dev/pandocore-prod/node-sensor/pando-node-sensor@sha256:a06b5bec9a6214fc1460fc195dec11f7c751dd5dc83872bb72fdfcd0c7de8a40

Verify the digest we publish, rather than whatever a repository listing hands back: untagged images that predate this pipeline can sit in the same repository, and they carry no signature to check. The same flag applies to cosign verify-attestation, which also needs --type spdxjson or --type cyclonedx to choose which bill of materials to read. We are not claiming publicly auditable signing, because we do not do it: you are verifying against the key above, not against a third-party log.

Signing and SBOMs cover all five images. Vulnerability scanning does not. Trivy gates the node sensor image on critical and high-severity findings that have a fix available, and blocks the build on them. The sidecar image is scanned but not gated. The controller, the admission webhook and the enforcer are not scanned at all. Extending that is outstanding work, and until it lands it would be wrong to let "we scan our images" stand without the qualifier.

Reporting a vulnerability

If you find a security issue, email eliot@pandocore.io. We welcome good-faith security research and will not pursue action against researchers who act in good faith, avoid privacy violations and service disruption, and give us a reasonable chance to fix an issue before disclosing it publicly. We are a small team, so we cannot promise a formal SLA, but we will acknowledge your report quickly and keep you posted. We do not run a paid bug bounty yet.

Compliance roadmap

We will be straight about this: PandoCore does not hold SOC 2 or any third-party security attestation today. We are targeting a SOC 2 Type 1 report in Q4 2027, with a Type 2 to follow shortly after.

In the meantime, the practices above are how we operate: a narrow Kubernetes grant with pod deletion withheld by name; a signed container supply chain with published SBOMs; a fail-open, monitor-by-default posture; and evidence egress you control.

The sensor's full grant is short enough to read in one sitting, and it is in the chart: read, list and watch on pods; create on events; create, delete and get on network policies; get on replica sets, stateful sets and daemon sets; list on PandoCore's own policy resource; list on services; and get on the kube-system namespace alone. Three verbs are withheld deliberately and by name, with the reasons written next to them: pods: delete, pods: patch and endpoints: get. Cluster-wide pod deletion is a trivially available denial of service, which is why the response ceiling is capped in code to match.

Our detection figures were re-measured on the node sensor. The earlier numbers came from the retired per-pod collector and do not carry over to a different architecture, so they were withdrawn rather than restated. The replacement measurement is zero false isolations across 3,966 engine-hours of sensors that were permitted to isolate, 1,231 of them on the exact build we ship. A zero bounds the rate rather than being one: at most one false isolation in 1,324 engine-hours, at 95 percent confidence. The product page carries the detail.

What changed, and what it cost

PandoCore used to ship as a sidecar: one detector container injected into every pod it protected. It now ships as a node sensor, one DaemonSet pod per node, alongside a cluster controller. The injected sidecar and its mutating admission webhook are deprecated and receive no further release. The node sensor plus the controller is the one supported install.

The cluster controller. A StatefulSet that receives evidence from sensors over mTLS and owns delivery to your SIEM, Slack and the portal. It runs unprivileged and holds no Kubernetes API permissions at all. It is documented in the controller docs.

The node sensor runs with more privilege than the sidecar did, and you should price that in. It is a DaemonSet with hostPID, running as root with CAP_SYS_PTRACE, mounting the host /proc read-only and a host path for its own state. The sidecar ran unprivileged inside your pod. This is real additional surface on your nodes, it is not a detail we would rather you missed, and it is the direct cost of reaching signals that a per-pod detector could not see across user boundaries.

Two platforms the sensor does not run on. GKE Autopilot rejects privileged host-PID pods unless they are allowlisted, and EKS Fargate runs no DaemonSets at all. The sidecar worked on GKE Autopilot and the node sensor does not. If you are on either, PandoCore has no install path for you today, and we would rather say so here than let you find out during a Helm install.

What you get back for all of that: one sensor pod per node instead of one container per pod, a resource reservation made once per node rather than multiplied by your pod count, no mutating admission webhook in the critical path of pod creation, and nothing injected into your workloads.
